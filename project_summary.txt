================================================================
  AP STATS FINAL PROJECT SUMMARY
  Does heavier recent workload hurt NBA player performance?
================================================================

------------------------------------------------------------
1. RESEARCH QUESTION
------------------------------------------------------------
Do NBA players perform worse in a game after a stretch of heavy
recent playing time?

  Explanatory variable (x):  Minutes played in the player's
                             previous 5 games (same season)
  Response variable    (y):  Game Score (GmSc) in the current game

Game Score is Basketball-Reference's box-score performance index
combining scoring efficiency, rebounds, assists, steals, blocks,
and turnovers into one number.


------------------------------------------------------------
2. DATA
------------------------------------------------------------
Source:    basketball-reference.com player game logs
Seasons:   2021-22, 2022-23, 2023-24 (three regular seasons)
Players:   Every NBA player who, in a given season,
           - averaged at least 20.0 MPG, AND
           - appeared in at least 60 games

Discovery: Scraped the per-game stats page for each season and
           filtered on the criteria above.
Gamelogs:  Pulled each qualifying player-season's full game log,
           keeping only games actually played (filtered out DNPs).

Final dataset:
  Total player-game rows fetched : 37,441
  Unique players                 : 290
  Player-seasons                 : 522
  Usable after 5-game window     : 34,831 player-games


------------------------------------------------------------
3. MODELS AND HYPOTHESES
------------------------------------------------------------
The fatigue hypothesis predicts a NEGATIVE relationship between
recent workload and current-game performance, so all tests are
ONE-SIDED.


----- Model A: Pooled Simple Linear Regression -----

   GmSc = b0 + b1 * MP_prev5 + e

   H0 : b1 = 0
   HA : b1 < 0
   Test: t-test on the slope of a simple linear regression
         (AP Stats Unit 9).

----- Model B: Within-Player Analysis (AP-scope) -----

   Step 1: For each of the 290 players (>= 50 games of data),
           fit a simple linear regression on THAT player's games
                GmSc_i = b0_i + b1_i * MP_prev5_i
           and record the player's slope b1_i.

   Step 2: One-sample t-test on the 290 slopes
                H0 : mu_slope = 0
                HA : mu_slope < 0   (one-sided)

   This isolates within-player variation. It answers "for the
   same player, does a heavier-than-usual recent stretch predict
   a worse-than-usual game?", which is what the fatigue
   hypothesis really asks.

   Both steps (simple linear regression, one-sample t-test) are
   in the AP Stats curriculum. Combining them is a standard
   multi-step application.


------------------------------------------------------------
4. RESULTS
------------------------------------------------------------

----- Model A: Pooled regression (n = 34,831) -----
   b0 (intercept)        : -3.7515
   b1 (slope)            : +0.10604  GmSc per extra minute
   SE(b1)                :  0.00131
   t statistic           : +80.93
   one-sided p (HA b1<0) :  ~ 1
   R^2                   :  0.158
   Pearson r             : +0.398

   The slope is large, positive, and highly significant
   in the OPPOSITE direction of the hypothesis.
   Decision: FAIL to reject H0.

----- Model B: Within-player slopes (n = 290 players) -----
   mean slope             : +0.0323 GmSc per minute
   sd of slopes           :  0.0541
   median slope           : +0.0370
   % of players negative  :  21.0%
   mean per-player r      : +0.111
   t statistic            : +10.15  (df = 289)
   one-sided p (HA mu<0)  :  ~ 1

   Even after stripping out between-player differences,
   the within-player slopes are predominantly positive.
   79% of players have a positive slope.
   Decision: FAIL to reject H0.


------------------------------------------------------------
5. CONCLUSION
------------------------------------------------------------
We fail to reject H0 under both models. The data give NO
evidence that heavier recent workload is associated with lower
Game Scores. In fact, both models find a clearly positive
relationship in the OPPOSITE direction.


------------------------------------------------------------
6. WHY THE HYPOTHESIS DIDN'T HOLD (discussion)
------------------------------------------------------------
Two lurking variables push the relationship in the positive
direction and likely swamp any real fatigue effect at the
5-game horizon:

  (a) Form / coach selection.
      Coaches give heavy minutes to players who are playing
      well. So a heavy 5-game stretch is a signal the player
      is "hot" right now, and form tends to persist. That
      yields a positive relationship between recent minutes
      and the next game's performance.

  (b) Injury / return-to-play asymmetry.
      Returning players are on minutes restrictions AND are
      rusty. Low recent minutes therefore often coincide with
      below-average performance, not above-average.

A genuine fatigue effect almost certainly exists in the NBA
(many studies of back-to-backs find one), but it would be
small relative to (a) and (b) at a 5-game window. Isolating it
would require controlling for back-to-backs, rest days, travel,
and injury status -- which is beyond the scope of AP Stats.


------------------------------------------------------------
7. CONDITIONS FOR INFERENCE
------------------------------------------------------------
Model A (regression t-test on slope):
  - Linearity:    binned-mean plot is roughly linear in this range.
  - Independence: imperfect (same player appears many times).
                  This is the central reason Model B is reported.
  - Normality of residuals: with n ~ 35,000 the CLT applies.
  - Equal variance: residual spread looks reasonable across x.

Model B (one-sample t-test on 290 slopes):
  - Independence between players: reasonable.
  - Sample size 290 makes the t distribution very close to
    normal; histogram of slopes is approximately symmetric
    around the mean.
